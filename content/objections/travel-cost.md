## The concern

This objection is usually accurate arithmetic, not a brush-off. For an international attendee the real number is pass + long-haul airfare + [PLACEHOLDER] 4-5 hotel nights + meals + days out of office — plausibly [PLACEHOLDER] $5,000-$8,000 all-in from LATAM, APJ, or EMEA. The person raising it often isn't the one who approves it; they're anticipating what their manager or finance team will say, and they may be embarrassed to say "we can't afford this" directly. Exchange-rate exposure (LATAM especially) and corporate travel freezes make it worse. Treat the number as real.

## Response approach

Start by agreeing with the premise: it *is* a significant investment, especially with international travel, and it deserves a real business case rather than hand-waving.

Then reframe from cost to comparison:

1. **Acknowledge**: "You're right — with flights and hotels this is a real investment, and I wouldn't suggest it if I didn't think it paid for itself."
2. **Total the alternatives**: what would it cost to get the same things separately — a training course, a paid architecture workshop, months of scheduling for an exec briefing? Oktane consolidates all of them into one trip.
3. **Shrink the number**: stack every applicable discount before quoting — early-bird pricing, [PLACEHOLDER] group rate at 3+ registrations, [PLACEHOLDER] account-team customer codes. Never present list price if a better defensible number exists.
4. **Remove their hardest step**: offer the [PLACEHOLDER] budget justification letter template so the champion doesn't have to write the business case themselves.
5. **Bundle**: suggest combining the trip with other US-side meetings ([PLACEHOLDER] HQ briefing, partner meetings) so the airfare serves multiple purposes.

## Reusable talking points

- "The pass is the smallest part of the cost — so let's make sure the trip does the most work. Three days can cover your roadmap questions, your architecture review, exec meetings, and hands-on training in one itinerary."
- "Compare it to what you're already planning to spend: one enterprise training seat costs [PLACEHOLDER] comparable money and delivers a fraction of this."
- "You're making [PLACEHOLDER] a major identity architecture decision this year. Against the cost of getting that wrong, this is cheap insurance."
- "If we register before [PLACEHOLDER] early-bird deadline, the price drops and — for teams paying in local currency — the dollar amount locks now."
- "I can send you a justification letter your manager can approve as-is. You shouldn't have to build the business case from scratch."
- "If you send three people, the group rate kicks in and you can actually cover the parallel tracks — the per-person value goes up, not down."

## What NOT to say

- Don't minimize the number ("it's really not that much") — for their budget it may be a lot, and dismissing it dismisses them.
- Don't lead with the pass price while hiding the trip cost; they will do the full math, and discovering it later feels like a bait-and-switch.
- Don't invent discounts or promise codes you haven't confirmed exist — verify against [PLACEHOLDER] the current pricing page first.
- Don't say "just expense it" — you don't know their policy, and it signals you haven't listened.
- For public sector: don't offer to cover or subsidize anything. See the pubsec region page — this isn't a discount conversation, it's an ethics rule.
